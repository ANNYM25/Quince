# Partner Playbook: the BCG Zurich Senior Partner's feedback, reverse-engineered

Source: a six-page review of a finalist case-competition deck, written by a Senior Partner at BCG Zurich. Treat it as the gold standard for how a top partner reads a deck. Each section below gives what the partner said, the rule underneath it, and how an agent checks it. Every reviewer and every council judge applies these rules.

## The partner's mental model, in one paragraph

A partner reads the titles first, in order, and decides in under a minute whether the deck has an argument. Then reads the executive summary as if it were the only slide. Then hunts for the weakest number, because that is where the Q&A will go. Analysis quality is assumed at this level; **the gap between a good deck and a winning deck is headlines and defensibility, not more analysis.** Strengths are protected as carefully as weaknesses are fixed.

---

## 1. The one-sentence test

**Said:** "The rubric's own test is whether a judge can summarise the deck in one sentence. Yours passes: 'Own the second purchase by moving up to $9M from rented to owned channels, with holdouts deciding every dollar.'"

**Rule:** The deck has a governing thought of one sentence with three parts: the move (own the second purchase), the scale (up to $9M, rented → owned), and the decision rule (holdouts decide). The presenter opens with it and closes with it.

**Check:** Write the sentence from the deck alone. If two reviewers write different sentences, the storyline is not clear enough.

## 2. Action titles: the one non-cosmetic edit

**Said:** "Only slides 2 and 3 pass the consulting test for a headline: a complete sentence, stating the conclusion, carrying the number, and arguable. The others name a topic or a tool." "Judges read titles first."

**Rule:** Every content title passes four tests: (1) complete sentence with a verb, (2) states the conclusion, not the topic, (3) carries the number that proves it, (4) arguable: a smart person could disagree. Failure modes the partner named:

| Failure | Partner's example | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| No number | Paid reach is getting too expensive to rely on | Rented reach costs 29% more than two years ago and keeps the fewest customers |
| Names the tool, not the finding | CJA reveals where the funnel is actually leaking | 77% of carts stall, and the 25% of buyers who engage make up 66% of repeaters |
| A question, not a claim | Why rivals can't copy Quince's model | Rivals can copy a discount overnight, but not factory-direct cost plus opted-in buyers |
| Topic label | Turning 3PL shipping events into real-time journeys | Identity is ready today; three gaps close for $4.8M inside the owned line |
| Fragment, no verb | The 90-day South pilot: 10% holdout, $4M on the line | A 90-day South pilot gates the first $4M move on holdout proof at month 3 |

**Check:** `headlines.md` tests, then the read-aloud test: "read the seven titles aloud in order. With the current set, the argument has holes; with the proposed set, it stands alone." Keep every title to one line where possible (the partner flagged a title that wrapped and gave the shorter version).

## 3. The executive summary carries a complication

**Said:** "Slide 2 is all answer. A strong executive summary carries Situation and Complication too, so it survives being read on its own and ties your answer back to the case question." The fix: one strip above the lever cards: "Viral growth built first buyers; CAC is up 29% to $62 and 90-day repeat sits at 24% against 30 to 35% at peers."

**Rule:** The exec summary = one SCQ strip (situation and complication with numbers, one line) + the answer (levers) + the result (metrics the case asks for). It must work if it is the only slide a reader sees.

**Check:** Cover the rest of the deck. Does slide 2 alone state the problem, the answer and the impact in the case's own metrics?

## 4. The hero stat must not be the weakest number

**Said:** "Add 'directional' under the 2.7× stat, or swap it for the 28.1% vs 4.7% engaged repeat, which rests on far more people. Appendix 11 already admits the week-2 cohorts are ~48 buyers; a hero stat should not be the weakest number in the deck."

**Rule:** The biggest number on a slide is the most robust one, not the most dramatic. Small-sample figures are labelled "directional" or demoted. The Integrity agent ranks every hero number by its base (n, source quality) and flags any hero that is weaker than a supporting number.

## 5. Definitions live in the card, not the footnote

**Said:** "Move the CAC definition out of the footnote. The case's own Exhibit J defines the 10 to 12% CAC reduction as blended... Say 'on Exhibit J's definition' in the card itself, and keep the strict new-customer figure (≈ −8%) ready for Q&A."

**Rule:** When a number depends on a definition a judge could dispute, the definition sits next to the number ("−12 to −14% blended CAC, on Exhibit J's definition"), and the stricter alternative is prepared (appendix or notes). Anchor to the client's own definitions wherever they exist.

## 6. Defensibility: every claim survives a 30-second question

**Said:** Twelve likely questions with prepared answers, e.g. "Your 2.7× headline comes from ~48 buyers a week. Why lead with it?", "Exhibit D says 22% vs 35%; CJA says 23.0 vs 23.4. Which is it?", "What if Gate 1 fails?", "Why pilot in the South?". "Rule for every answer: number first, source second, implication third."

**Rule:** For each core slide, the Integrity agent and the BCG judge write the two hardest questions a partner would ask and check that the deck (slide, notes or appendix) holds the answer. Reconcile conflicting sources on the slide or in the appendix ("different windows and populations; both say retargeting earns no repeat premium"). Every claim in the deck can be stated as number → source → implication.

## 7. Breadth stays, but each slide has one focal point

**Said:** "Slides 3, 4 and 7 each carry three or four messages. Do not cut them (the rubric wants the breadth), but the presenter points at one element and ignores the rest. The audience follows the finger."

**Rule:** Exhaustive content is correct; the fix for a busy slide is a clear focal element (accent colour, largest number, takeaway bar), not deleting evidence. Every slide plan names its focal element. This is why this skill's density standard is "minimal but exhaustive".

## 8. Precision details a partner notices

**Said (housekeeping list):**
- "'Influence r $16M' breaks mid-word in the budget bar. Widen the segment or abbreviate to 'Infl. $16M'." → No label may break mid-word or overflow its shape.
- "Define the y-axis. 'Owned relationship' needs a unit, e.g. 'share of buyers reachable via app, email or SMS'." → Every axis carries a unit or a definition.
- "Reduce type sizes from six to three (title, body, footnote). The source lines are below legible size when projected." → Three working sizes; sources at 8pt minimum.
- "The 'Target 30%' marker crowds the 31% bar and makes the base case look marginal. Move the label or add the range as a whisker." → Annotations never crowd the data they annotate; show ranges as ranges.
- "Replace the ★ and ✓ glyphs with plain bold text; they read as decorative." → No decorative glyphs.
- "Files carry the team number only... Confirm no university name or team name appears anywhere, including file metadata." → Naming and metadata hygiene.
- "Export the PDF after any edit and check that the fonts embed." → Re-export and re-check after every change.

## 9. What not to touch: protect the strengths

**Said:** "These are the strengths that put you in contention. Do not dilute them while fixing the above."
1. The governing thought and three levers that are "mutually exclusive and together cover the problem: capture the relationship, activate it in the golden month, stop paying for it twice."
2. A decision rule that resolves the case's own objection: "Making Option C the referee for Options A and B is the most sophisticated move in the deck."
3. Taking a position: "Zero discounts. The rules permit narrow offers; choosing none is a position, and it differentiates you."
4. "Gates with pass marks, owners and funding sources... Most teams present a roadmap; you present a contract."
5. "Every number traced to case, CJA or public source, with derivations... your best defence in Q&A."
6. "Sourcing on every slide, consistent footers, restrained palette, no clip art."

**Rule:** Each review round lists the deck's strengths before its fixes, and no fix may weaken a listed strength. Patterns to build into any strategy deck:
- **MECE levers** that read as a sequence (capture → activate → stop waste).
- **A referee:** turn the client's biggest objection (here: "attribution can't be trusted") into the decision rule (holdouts decide).
- **A position:** one deliberate "we will not" that rivals would hedge on.
- **A contract, not a roadmap:** next steps with pass marks, owners, timing and funding source, tied to the case's metrics.
- **A number ledger:** an appendix slide tracing every number to its source and derivation.

## 10. Delivery rules that shape the deck

**Said:** "Open with the one-sentence answer, not with 'thank you for having us.' Close slide 7 by repeating it." "Whoever presents slide 4 must be able to derive the funnel numbers without looking." "Appendix is for Q&A only. Know the page numbers."

**Rule:** The closing slide repeats the governing thought. Speaker notes for each core slide start with the one thing to say and the numbers the presenter must be able to derive. The appendix opener lists page numbers by topic (CJA evidence, backup analysis, defence) so any answer is one click away.
